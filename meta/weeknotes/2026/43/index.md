# 2026 - Week 43

## Librarian of the Week / pleasing Aaron Swartz

On Wednesday of this week, our own team of crack librarians met with a second team of crack librarians, also from the House of Commons Library. That second team do sterling work compiling current awareness bulletins - or what is going on in the world in assorted area of expertise - for our crack team of researchers. All of which is managed by means of an [RSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSS) aggregation service. Which works just fine for lots of externally published material. More publishers still churning out RSS feeds than most of us might imagine. Even if those feeds are not always well advertised. Problems arise when also trying to aggregate material published by Parliament, where are own websites don't offer much in the way of alerts or content syndication.

Happily, Young Robert and [Michael](https://bsky.app/profile/fantasticlife.bsky.social) are of an age where publishing RSS is just part of what one does. So, over the years, they've cobbled together a variety of prototype applications, from our [Procedure Browsable Space](https://api.parliament.uk/procedure-browser)&trade; to our [Bill Papers](https://api.parliament.uk/bill-papers)&trade; application, all churning out RSS feeds alongside what what Robert would undoubtedly call [other applicable representations of information resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/REST). All of those prototypes aimed squarely at making life easier for team:[Anya](https://bsky.app/profile/anyaso.bsky.social). Though now finding a second life of usefulness in the hands of the current awareness librarians. If you've used our feeds and found them helpful, why not get in touch and let us know? We're always grateful for a pat on the head or a scratch behind the ears.

Even more happily, we've been putting a hell of a lot of work into [new, old Parliamentary Search](https://beta-search.parliament.uk/), allowing users both inside and outside Parliament to search across Parliament-published material using a whole myriad of facets and filters. If you've used new, old Parliamentary Search, you'll already be aware that its default - and, for now, only - result list ordering is latest first. Each result coming complete with a title, a description, a date and a link. At which point, our dear reader will probably be thinking, what better candidate for [the old RSS treatment](https://www.rssboard.org/rss-specification)? You would not be alone. Librarian Anya asked much the same question as she pondered how long it might take to add RSS views of search result listings. About two hours, said Michael, promptly throwing poor Developer Jon under the delivery bus.

Two days later - Jon has much higher standards than Michael, and likes to ensure everything has been properly thought through and has [regression tests in place](https://github.com/ukparliament/search-prototype/tree/main/spec) - new, old Parliamentary Search now comes complete with RSS feeds. Until they've been throughly tested by our own crack team of librarians and thoroughly tested by the current awareness team of crack librarians, we are not yet advertising them from the HTML views. If you want to try them out, just [run a search](https://beta-search.parliament.uk/search?filter%5Btype_sesrollup%5D%5B%5D=90996&query=canada), pop '.rss' between the '/search' and the '?' in the URL bar and you too can have [all the RSS for all the parliamentary material you're ever likely to need](https://beta-search.parliament.uk/search.rss?filter%5Btype_sesrollup%5D%5B%5D=90996&query=canada).

Anya also takes the opportunity to point out that occasionally - particularly during [recess](https://ukparliament.github.io/ontologies/time-period/time-period-ontology#d4e282) and [prorogation periods](https://ukparliament.github.io/ontologies/time-period/time-period-ontology#d4e256) - her own team are not always aware of new material arriving down the pipes in dire need of subject indexing, procedural indexing and interlinking. This because the current ingest engine lacks anything in way of what Robert might call 'observability'. She adds that Jon's new RSS feeds may also prove useful for her own crack team of librarians. Simply subscribe to a search for a given type of material, and get alerted every time a new one drops.

For responding so readily to customer needs and doing so in his usual professional manner, this week Developer Jon is the lucky recipient of our much coveted Librarian of the Week award. Also, for having worked with us once, experienced the chaos, and choosing to return to the fold. Not many would have been so brave.

Our dear reader will hopefully forgive us for continuing this section with yet another paragraph or two, but this is exactly the kind of work that always proves most pleasing. To come across a colleague with a long-standing problem that you're able to help solve with the very minimum of effort. Not because you're cutting corners, but because you've already done most of the hard work to make the easy stuff possible. And because, a good couple of decades ago, a handful of right-thinking people took the time and trouble to standardise an approach to content syndication that worked with the weave of the web. Lovely stuff.

It should be pointed out that [these very notes also come with their own RSS feed](https://ukparliament.github.io/ontologies/meta/weeknotes/feed.rss), so if you're ever left thinking, "I've not seen one of Anya and Michael's massive walls of text in quite a while", why not treat yourself and subscribe.

## All the other new, old Parliamentary Search news direct to your reading application

In other new, old Parliamentary Search news, Developer Jon remains busy fettling aliases, two stacks now signed off by the librarians and ready to go live. He's also fixed a small bug arising from the case sensitivity of aliases, Librarian Jayne noticing that production search did not care a fig for casing but Jon's code does. Or at least did. It [now works for both Datecertified and datecertified](https://trello.com/c/XPFHjB9o/907-case-sensitivity-of-the-alias-itself-not-what-the-user-is-searching-for). Or will do when the alias code is released. Which we're hoping will be quite soon.

Librarian Phil also noticed a discrepancy between [what production search defines as a week and what Jon's search defines as a week](https://trello.com/c/SziZuase/905-thisweek-and-lastweek-relative-date-ranges). The old code insisting that a week runs from Sunday to Saturday, Jon's code preferring a Monday to Sunday definition. This card hit the weeknotes pile with no work necessary, it being decided that Monday to Sunday was correct in all eyes except Michael's. If it's [good enough for the International Standards Organisation](https://isoweeks.com/#descriptionTab), it's quite good enough for us.

Another card requiring no effort arrived in the form of yet another discrepancy. It turns out that when attempting to return results for items with a Boolean set to false, old, old search also managed to include items with the same Boolean having a value of NULL. So not exactly FALSE, more NOT TRUE. Another thing that Jon has managed to fix in new, old search without really trying.

## Dashboarding the Wardley maps

If our dear reader can remember as far back as season 27 of these notes, they may well remember that [Delivery Manager Lydia](https://bsky.app/profile/lydiakeegal.bsky.social) was dragging our two resident geriatrics along on a [Wardley mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wardley_map) exercise of all our procedural data integration systems. The resulting maps have proved quite useful, particularly in paper format. Every planning day we take all of our pixels down to the Library's plotter printer, print them up at a size even Robert and Michael's aging eyes can see, plaster them to the walls, and scribble all over them.

Which is all fine and all good, but at least some of us work in a 'data team' and 'data team's tend to be less accepting of big bits of paper than we might be. With that in mind we have collated all of our pixel-based nodes and all of our pixel-based edges into data, and, with much needed help from Data Scientist Eva and Data Scientist Catherine, popped all of that data into a shiny new [Shiny](https://rstudio.github.io/shinydashboard/) Dashboard. Which is now coming close to production ready, giving us the ability to click on any given node, follow the edges upwards and build a picture of every system that we'd lose should we happen to lose the one that we clicked on. Impressive if slightly terrifying.

As if that pause for thought were not enough, we're here to offer one more. Whilst we have no problems with Wardley maps per se, we do find some of the rhetoric accompanying them slightly icky. There is a belief amongst some circles that everything eventually tends toward commodity. A belief undermined by an old story from [Mr Sheridan](https://bsky.app/profile/johnlsheridan.bsky.social) that we repeat often and with relish. At some point in recent history, there was a certain brand of tape head - used by our friends at [The National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/) - that was indeed an off-the-shelf commodity component. At least until the Fukushima earthquake and subsequent nuclear disaster took the one factory manufacturing said tape heads offline. At which point, the kind of thing one could have almost ordered at Tandy became rarer than gold. Or indeed gnat's teeth. So however good your map making skills, if you glance at the bit down the bottom and think that's just commodity, we should probably outsource it, there's every chance you might be proved wrong.

There is something about Wardley maps that brings out the nostalgist in all of us. They do tend to point to a certain stage of history when globalisation was well established and seemingly under little threat. Also a point when [the fragility of the modern web](https://xkcd.com/2347/) was less apparent to all but the geekiest amongst us. Seen from the point of view of 2026, "why not just use some other people's computers and move to the cloud" seems more than a little naive. But then such thoughts are well above our particular pay grades - and this is weeknotes, not Gartner - so we'll park those reckons here. If anyone out there has given any consideration to what a value chain map might look like in a world that appears to be retreating from globalisation, why not get in touch? Very occasionally, people do actually do that, and we're always delighted.

## Subjects and specialists

Team:thesaurus continue to deal with feedback from Commons Library researchers, based on their initial exposure to our Library Knowledge Base&trade; in its current Subject Specialist Finder&trade; guise. The past few weeks have seen updates to labels, alternative labels, scope notes and taxonomic position of subjects as far and wide as [Court orders](https://trello.com/c/RWQf04KE/490-court-orders), [family law](https://trello.com/c/PHoWMTgp/489-family-law), [famine](https://trello.com/c/ybfY7vWQ/466-famine), [Gurkhas](https://trello.com/c/t5qsKJjy/484-gurkhas), [medical equipment](https://trello.com/c/go2KiOQk/445-medical-equipment) and [natural diasters](https://trello.com/c/PzLMn1ge/465-natural-disasters). That team have also issued [a whole slew of changes pertaining to matters in and around climate science, net zero and climate change adaptation](https://trello.com/c/xRhlbXW5/457-climate-science-net-zero-climate-change-adaptation-adaptation).

Given that the Library Knowledge Base is the first service that's ever exposed the taxonomic structure of the thesaurus to end users, Librarian Ned has also proposed [a new policy](https://trello.com/c/S1KsGrN8/480-controlling-the-kind-of-ids-we-used-to-put-under-scaffolding-terms) on what our crack team of librarians refer to as 'scaffolding terms'. Or concepts in the thesaurus that are not intended for use by indexers, but exist purely as managerial holding pens for related concepts that are. That policy proposal being met by applause from across our librarian community.

Outside of Library Knowledge Base&trade; usage, there has also been an update to [the planning concept](https://trello.com/c/HiNl0b3j/437-planning) and - where disambiguation is required - entities associated with the devolved legislatures and governments [now use the full name of the legislature or government](https://trello.com/c/P25TdalU/365-jp-and-eb-unpicking-topics-and-subjects-in-search-24-25-session) and not the rather confusing acronyms we had been using.

Back on data tidying duties, we are pleased to report that all records that should be indexed with the closure motion concept [are now indexed with the closure motion concept](https://trello.com/c/p4ddid9k/470-closure-motions-procedural-term). Martin meanwhile has been exercising his librarian chops on affirmative instruments approved by the House of Commons. When this happens, the Journal Office should add the date of approval to their systems, which then flows throw the pipes and into our systems. But for reasons unknown, 140 had slipped through the net with no approval date applied. Martin hooked some bait to his rod and went off on a fishing trip. 140 affirmative instruments were landed, all of which [now have their House of Commons approval date in place](https://trello.com/c/KfQnVwOW/464-commons-approved-date-on-affirmative-papers). Excellent catch, Librarian Martin.

Librarian Emily has been concentrating her tidying efforts on all the places we'd somehow used Henry VIII clauses as a procedural term, despite Henry VIII clauses not being a procedure. These have also [now been tidied](https://trello.com/c/oRESXeJb/100-henry-viii-clauses).

Not to be left out, Librarian Ayesha has been [decluttering our motion records](https://trello.com/c/onzgu1jg/474-check-to-see-if-motion-lapsed-needs-to-be-added-to-summary), sorting those that lapsed from those approved. Neat.
